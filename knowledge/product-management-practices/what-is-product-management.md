# What Is Product Management?

The product manager role sits at the intersection of business, technology, and
user experience. Practitioners define the role from different angles — but
they converge on a core: the PM is responsible for discovering what users need
and what the business can sustainably deliver, then aligning the team to build
it.

## The Role Definition Spectrum

| Framing | Author | Core claim |
|---------|--------|------------|
| The mini-CEO | Josh Elman, Ken Yeung | The PM owns the product's direction and outcomes, not just features |
| The discovery person | Martin Eriksonn | The PM discovers what users need and what the business can deliver |
| The alignment person | Sachin Rekhi | The PM aligns teams around a shared understanding of the customer and the business |
| What it is *not* | Marty Cagan | PM is not project management, not product marketing, not engineering management |

## What Product Management Is Not

Marty Cagan's two essays are the canonical boundary-setting references. The
first clarifies that PM is not project management, not product marketing, and
not engineering management — it is a distinct discipline with its own
accountabilities.[^cagan-pm-is-not] The second draws the PM vs product
marketing line: PM owns what to build and why; product marketing owns how to
take it to market.[^cagan-pm-vs-pmm]

## The Evolution of the Role

Ellen Chisa traces how the PM role has evolved from a project-delivery
function to a discovery-and-strategy function.[^chisa-evolution] Mat Balez
frames the PM's identity challenge — "Product Manager You Are..." — as a call
to own the role rather than let it be defined by the
organization.[^balez-pm-you-are]

## Day-to-Day Reality

Kevin Wei's "Day in the Life" contrasts consumer and enterprise PM work,
showing how the same role manifests differently depending on the
audience.[^wei-day-in-life] Julia Austin's HBR piece distills what it takes
to be a great PM across these contexts.[^austin-great-pm]
