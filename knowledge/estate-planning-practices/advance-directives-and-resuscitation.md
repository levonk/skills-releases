# Advance Directives and Resuscitation

## The Three Layers

Advance directives only matter when you cannot communicate your own
wishes.[^nia-affairs] They exist in three layers that people routinely
conflate:

| Document | What it is | When it applies |
|----------|-----------|-----------------|
| **Living will** | Legal document — your written treatment preferences | Terminal/unconscious states, per its terms |
| **DNR order** | Medical order — "do not attempt CPR" | Cardiac/respiratory arrest, anywhere it's on file |
| **POLST / MOLST** | Medical order set — full treatment vs select vs comfort-focused | Serious illness; travels across settings |

A living will is *guidance*; DNR and POLST are *orders* — clinicians
act on orders in the moment. For seriously ill patients, the POLST
(Physician Orders for Life-Sustaining Treatment, MOLST/MOST/POST
depending on state) converts wishes into an actionable medical order
signed by a clinician.[^acs-advance-directives][^statpearls-ad]

## The Resuscitation Decision

The user's question — "whether resuscitation efforts should be made" —
deserves an honest framing:

- **Full treatment** — all effective interventions: CPR, ICU,
  ventilator.
- **Selective/limited treatment** — restore function where possible
  but avoid ICU-level escalation and many resuscitation efforts.
- **Comfort-focused** — symptom relief only; allow natural death;
  hospital only if comfort can't be achieved at home.

POLST forms make this a checkbox decision instead of a crisis
guess.[^acs-advance-directives] Key consideration for cancer patients:
**CPR outcomes in advanced illness are poor** — resuscitation in
frail, advanced-disease patients rarely restores meaningful life and
often adds traumatic last days. This is a values decision best made
calmly, in writing, and discussed with both the
[healthcare proxy](powers-of-attorney.md) and the care team.

A DNR is **not** required for hospice enrollment, but hospice
patients who want full-code status need their goals-of-care
conversation clarified — see
[Palliative Care and Hospice](https://github.com/levonk/skills-releases/blob/main/knowledge/cancer-patient-practices/palliative-care-and-hospice.md).

## What a Living Will Covers

Per ACS:[^acs-advance-directives]

- Whether to use life-prolonging equipment (ventilator, dialysis)
- Do-not-resuscitate instructions
- Artificial nutrition/hydration (feeding tube, IV fluids)
- Palliative/comfort care preferences — you can decline
  life-prolonging measures and still direct full pain management
- Organ and tissue donation wishes

## Practical Points

- **State forms are free.** Most states publish approved advance
  directive forms; witnessing/notarization requirements vary by
  state. An attorney is helpful but not strictly required for the
  directive itself.
- **Give copies to people, not just file cabinets.** The healthcare
  proxy, the care team, and the hospital chart should all hold it.
  A POLST belongs on the refrigerator door or its local equivalent —
  EMTs look there.
- **Review after prognosis changes.** A directive written when healthy
  may not reflect wishes after a terminal diagnosis — reread it.

## Common Mistakes

- **Thinking a living will covers emergencies.** EMTs follow standing
  protocols, not documents in a drawer — a DNR/POLST is what they act
  on.
- **Leaving the proxy uninformed.** The document names the decider;
  the *conversation* is what lets them actually decide.
- **Conflating DNR with "no care."** DNR = no CPR. Pain relief,
  oxygen, and comfort measures continue unless separately declined.
