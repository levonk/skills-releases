# Treatment Decision Making

## The Core Practice

The decision to accept a treatment plan should reflect your
preferences, values, and goals — made *after* understanding the
options and their likely outcomes.[^acs-questions] Three practices
make that understanding real rather than nominal:

### 1. Ask the volume and complication questions

For any recommended surgery or procedure:

- "How many of these surgeries do you perform a year?"
- "What is your complication rate, and what are the most common
  complications?"
- "What is the realistic range of recovery — best case, typical,
  worst?"

Surgeon volume correlates with outcomes for many procedures. These
questions are routine at major cancer centers and legitimate
everywhere.

### 2. Talk to someone who has had the procedure

The seed author's regret: he did not talk to a patient who had
undergone his surgery, and the physical reality was much rougher than
he expected.[^maxwell-cancer-2026] Surgeons explain procedures;
patients explain what it *feels like* and what recovery actually
demands. Ask the care team, disease-specific foundations (see
[Patient Advocacy and Support](patient-advocacy-and-support.md)), or
support groups to connect you.

### 3. Walk the alternative branches

For each option on the standard-of-care tree (see
[Standard of Care Navigation](standard-of-care-navigation.md)), ask:

- "What happens if I do nothing?" — every NCCN patient question list
  includes this.[^nccn-patient-questions]
- "Does any option offer cure vs. long-term control vs. comfort?"
- "How do my age, overall health, and other conditions change the
  recommendation?"
- "How soon must I decide?" — some decisions have real deadlines;
  many don't.
- "How will we know the treatment is working?" — define the
  measurement *before* starting.

## Consent Reality Check

Informed consent documents enumerate risks; they rarely convey
frequency or lived severity. The volume/complication questions and the
past-patient conversation close that gap. If a recommended treatment
is also available at a higher-volume center, that is itself
decision-relevant — see
[Second Opinions and Cancer Centers](second-opinions-and-cancer-centers.md).

## Common Mistakes

- **Conflating urgency with rushing.** Even urgent cases usually allow
  days for a second opinion — ask "how much time do I have to decide?"
- **Not asking about non-treatment.** "What if I do nothing" is a
  legitimate branch; some patients reasonably choose less treatment.
- **Skipping the lived-experience check.** Medical descriptions of
  "moderate discomfort" can mean weeks of feeding tubes — find out
  from someone who went through it.
