# First Steps After Diagnosis

The first days after a cancer diagnosis feel chaotic. The goal of this
concept is a short, ordered list that converts shock into motion.

## Confirm What You Actually Know

Before doing anything else, get the diagnosis facts in writing:

- **Cancer type and subtype** — the exact pathology name (e.g.,
  "squamous cell carcinoma"), not just "cancer."
- **Stage** — or, if not yet staged, which tests will establish stage
  and when they are scheduled.
- **Where** — primary site and any known spread.
- **The pathology report** — request a copy. It is the foundational
  document every future consult will ask for.

Questions worth asking at the first oncology visit
(per NCI[^nci-questions]): What type of cancer do I have? What is the
stage? Has it spread? What further tests are needed before treatment?
Which doctors do I need to see? Will you help me get a second opinion?

## Start the Records System Immediately

Create a single folder (physical or digital) for everything:
appointment notes, pathology reports, imaging, lab results, insurance
correspondence. After each appointment, dictate what happened and what
the doctor said — use AI to turn the dictation into a dated summary
with open questions for next time.[^maxwell-cancer-2026] See
[Medical Record Keeping](medical-record-keeping.md).

## Identify the Care Team — and Its Gaps

Cancer care is delivered by a team that assembles over time: the
diagnosing physician, surgeon, medical oncologist, radiation
oncologist, pathologist, nurses, and often a social worker or patient
navigator. Write down names, roles, phone numbers, and portal
addresses. Note who the *coordinator* is — if nobody is coordinating,
that job defaults to you.

## Triage Urgency Honestly

Most cancers allow days-to-weeks for a second opinion without
changing the outcome — but some do not. Ask the diagnosing physician
directly: "How soon does treatment need to start, and what happens if
it is delayed two weeks?" Use the answer to set the pace for
[Second Opinions and Cancer Centers](second-opinions-and-cancer-centers.md).

## Book the Long-Lead Items in Parallel

Do not run the first weeks sequentially. In parallel:

1. Start the second-opinion appointment process at a major cancer
   center (records transfer takes days).
2. Request all records: pathology slides/blocks (not just the report),
   imaging on disc or portal export, lab results.
3. Push for [molecular and genetic testing](genetic-and-biomarker-testing.md)
   if not already ordered.
4. Notify insurance and ask for a case manager (see
   [Insurance and Financial Toxicity](insurance-and-financial-toxicity.md)).
5. Tell one organized friend or family member who can make calls —
   scheduling persistence is easier to delegate than to sustain alone
   while in shock (see [Scheduling Persistence](scheduling-persistence.md)).

## Common Mistakes

- **Waiting for the system to call you.** It often won't. Every
  promised referral, test order, and callback goes on your own list
  with a follow-up date.
- **Anchoring on the first plan.** Even a good first plan deserves a
  second read — see
  [Second Opinions and Cancer Centers](second-opinions-and-cancer-centers.md).
- **Deferring "paperwork."** Estate and directive questions feel
  morbid on day one; they are much harder later. A light pass through
  the sibling
  [estate-planning-practices](../estate-planning-practices/index.md)
  bundle early is cheaper than a crash effort during treatment.
