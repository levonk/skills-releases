# Core Estate Documents

## The Will

A will specifies how your estate — property, money, and other assets
held in your name — is distributed and managed at death. It also
covers what trusts cannot: **guardianship of minor children or
dependents**, care of pets, and funeral/memorial
wishes.[^nia-affairs]

Key roles inside a will:

- **Executor** — the person who administers the estate: gathers
  assets, pays debts, files the will with probate court, distributes
  what's left. Choose someone organized, willing, and geographically
  practical; name an alternate.
- **Guardian** — for minor children; arguably the most consequential
  choice in the document.
- **Beneficiaries** — who gets what; be specific for anything with
  sentimental or dispute potential.

**Without a will**, state intestacy law distributes the estate — a
default that ignores unmarried partners, friends, charities, and your
actual preferences, and adds court overhead.[^nia-affairs]

## The Revocable Living Trust

A living trust is a legal entity that holds your property while you're
alive (you control it fully) and distributes it at death under a
successor trustee — **outside probate**.[^freewill-checklist] In many
states it is the central document of the estate plan.[^kiplinger-docs]

Practical advantages:

- **Avoids probate** — faster distribution, lower cost, private
  (probate is a public record; a trust is not).
- **Incapacity continuity** — the successor trustee can manage trust
  assets if you're incapacitated, complementing the
  [financial POA](powers-of-attorney.md).

The catch: **a trust only covers what you put in it.** After signing,
you must retitle assets (house, accounts) into the trust — "funding
the trust." An unfunded trust is an expensive empty vessel. You still
need a will — a "pour-over" will catches anything left out.

## Will + Trust, Not Will vs. Trust

The standard middle-class arrangement: revocable living trust for the
major assets (home, brokerage) plus a simple will for guardianship,
personal items, and the pour-over backstop. Simple estates with few
assets may need only a will; an estate attorney is worth one consult
to decide.

## Common Mistakes

- **Creating the trust and never funding it.** Retitle the assets or
  the probate avoidance never happens.
- **Forgetting beneficiary designations.** Retirement accounts and
  life insurance pass by their own designation forms — outside the
  will entirely. See
  [Digital Estate and Beneficiaries](digital-estate-and-beneficiaries.md).
- **Naming an executor without asking them.** The role is real work;
  confirm willingness and tell them where documents live (see
  [Documentation and Letter of Instruction](documentation-and-letter-of-instruction.md)).
- **Set-and-forget.** Revisit after marriage, divorce, births, deaths
  of named agents, major asset changes, or a move to a new state.
